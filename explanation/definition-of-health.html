Definition of the state of health

Status: provisional — version 0.1, 18 October 2026. This definition is expected to change as the project learns what is measurable and what matters. Each revision records its trigger and its changes. See Revision process.

Why provisional?

The project treats the state of health as dynamic and nonstationary (Health is not stationary): the thing being defined changes over time, and so does our understanding of it. Fixing a definition permanently would contradict that premise. Instead the definition is versioned — a working commitment that is good enough to decide what to measure now, and explicit enough that revisions are visible rather than silent.

The expectation is that the data landscape (milestone M2 in the roadmap) will force revisions: some domains will prove measurable, others will not; some proxies will turn out to track the construct poorly.

The definition (v0.1)

Note

The state of health of a population is the joint condition of its members across the domains of mortality, physical morbidity, mental health, functional status, subjective wellbeing, and social wellbeing, at a given time and place — including how that condition is distributed within the population and how it is changing over time.

Three features of the wording carry weight:

  • Joint condition. The domains are not interchangeable and are not summed into a single number by definition. Any single number is an index built on top of the definition, not the definition itself.
  • Distribution. The state of health is a distribution, not an average — inequalities are part of the state of health, not an add-on.
  • Changing. The state of health is a trajectory, not a snapshot. From a decision perspective, we care about the cumulative (time-integrated) state of health over time — so actions are evaluated by their effect on cumulative outcomes, not only immediate levels.

Conceptual basis

The definition builds on three established positions:

  • WHO’s breadth. Health is “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity” (World Health Organization 1948). This is why the definition has six domains rather than one, and why clinical status alone is insufficient.
  • The ICF’s measurability. The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health treats functioning and disability as outcomes of the interaction between health conditions and contextual factors, spanning body functions and structures, activities, and participation (World Health Organization 2001). This is what makes functional status a domain in its own right rather than a by-product of disease.
  • The capability approach’s evaluative anchor. Health matters because of what it enables people to do and be (Sen 1999) — which is why subjective and social wellbeing are in scope, not just biology.

Domains

Domain What it covers Details
Mortality Premature and overall death, cause-of-death burden Measurement framework
Physical morbidity Disease incidence and prevalence, chronic conditions, injury Measurement framework
Mental health Mental health status and substance-use harms Measurement framework
Functional status Functioning, disability, activities of daily living Measurement framework
Subjective wellbeing Self-rated health, life satisfaction, positive wellbeing Measurement framework
Social wellbeing Social connection, support, and participation Measurement framework

Candidate additions. Oral health, sensory health (vision and hearing), and injury are currently folded into physical morbidity and functional status. They may be promoted to domains if the data landscape shows they can be measured distinctly and add information the current domains miss. The bar for adding a domain is that it must not merely duplicate an existing one.

Cross-cutting dimensions. Age and life-course stage, sex and gender, Indigenous identity, and geography apply to every domain rather than being domains themselves. Socioeconomic position is also used to stratify results — but as a determinant, never as a component of health (see Boundaries).

Proxies are not the state of health

This is the project’s most important measurement discipline. The state of health is a construct: real, but not directly observable. Every number we publish is a proxy — a measurement chosen because it tracks the construct, built by someone else, for some other purpose, with its own error.

Confusing a proxy for the construct is the central failure mode this project guards against. Consequences of the distinction:

  • No proxy defines its domain. Life expectancy does not define mortality’s contribution to health; it proxies it. Self-rated health does not define wellbeing.
  • Proxies carry two kinds of error. Ordinary measurement error, and construct error — the proxy may be reliable but track the wrong thing.
  • Proxies drift. Administrative definitions, coding practices, and survey instruments change over time, so a series can move because the proxy changed, not the health (nonstationarity).
  • Proxy disagreement is signal. When two proxies for the same construct disagree, the right response is to investigate why, not to average the disagreement away.
  • Indices are models. Any composite “health index” is a model built on chosen proxies and weights — a claim to be defended, not a measurement to be read off.
  • Causal analyses inherit all of this. In milestones M4–M5 the proxies are variables, and their proxy status is part of the assumptions register.

Every indicator page must therefore state the construct it proxies, the gap between proxy and construct, and the known limitations.

Boundaries

The definition deliberately excludes:

  • Determinants of health — income, education, housing, environment, service access. These enter analyses as explanatory variables and as stratification dimensions, never as components of health itself.
  • Health-system activity — wait times, procedures, spending, staffing. These are inputs and system performance, not the population’s state of health (they may explain it, which is a different role).
  • The absence of disease as sufficient. The absence of disease is not full health (World Health Organization 1948).
  • Any equivalence between self-reported and objective measures. Both are proxies; neither is the truth.

Resolution and fusion

The project uses all available levels of resolution, from individual to provincial and beyond:

  • individual (where microdata access and governance allow — see Why BC only? and the roadmap’s governance principles);
  • neighbourhood and local health area (LHA);
  • health service delivery area (HSDA) and health authority;
  • provincial;
  • external / non-BC (external drivers).

Different resolutions answer different questions and expose different things: fine resolutions show local heterogeneity that coarse summaries hide (and comparing coarse summaries to fine ones invites the ecological fallacy). The project therefore:

  1. records the resolution of every datum and every result;
  2. harmonises across resolutions where definitions are compatible;
  3. fuses pictures where that is useful, using multilevel/hierarchical models and other combination methods (Gelman and Hill 2007);
  4. treats any fused picture as a model output — reported with its assumptions and uncertainty, never as a fact.

Temporal semantics

  • Coverage targets as far back as sources allow, with each series carrying its observation window and definitional breaks handled explicitly.
  • Health is represented as levels, slopes, and discontinuities — a trajectory, as set out in Health is not stationary.
  • The definition itself is versioned; the thing defined drifts, and so does the definition.

Boundaries of the definition itself

This document defines the state of health. It deliberately does not yet settle: the exact indicator set (grows with the data landscape, M2), the weights of any future index (a modelling choice, M3+), or the causal claims that explain the state of health (M4–M5). Being honest about what a definition does not cover is part of the definition.

Revision process

The definition is revised when:

  • the data landscape (M2) shows a domain is unmeasurable as conceived, or a measurable domain is missing;
  • a proxy is shown to track its construct poorly;
  • new evidence establishes a domain, or collapses a distinction;
  • the population or measurement context shifts enough to invalidate part of the definition.

Each revision: increments the version, records the trigger and the change, updates the measurement framework and glossary as needed, and is reviewed by at least one domain reviewer (roadmap M1 acceptance criterion).
