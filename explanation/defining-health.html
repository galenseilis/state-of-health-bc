Defining health

Status: outline. The project’s provisional definition now lives at Definition of the state of health (milestone M1 in the roadmap); this page records the problem that definition has to solve.

Why define health first?

“State of health” sounds like something you could just measure. It is not. Before any indicator can be collected, compared over time, or used in a causal model, the project must commit to what counts as health and what does not — otherwise every downstream number rests on a different unstated assumption.

A definition is doing real work here. It determines:

  • What gets measured. Longevity only? Mental health? Functional capacity? Subjective wellbeing? Each choice admits some indicators and excludes others.
  • What comparisons mean. Two regions’ “health” are only comparable if both were measured against the same definition.
  • What the causal analysis is of. A causal model of health outcomes is undefined until the outcome is defined.

What the definition must pin down

The definition document (M1) will commit to:

  1. Conceptual basis — which established frameworks the definition builds on — e.g. the WHO definition of health as “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being” (World Health Organization 1948), the ICF functioning model (World Health Organization 2001), capability approaches (Sen 1999) — and why.
  2. Domains — the set of dimensions counted as part of the state of health (candidate families: mortality, morbidity, mental health, functional status, and context kept strictly separate from health itself).
  3. Boundaries — what is explicitly excluded, and why (e.g. determinants of health are not health; health-system inputs are not health).
  4. Unit of analysis — population-level vs. individual-level, and the geographic granularity the project works at.
  5. Temporal semantics — health as a time-varying state: a trajectory with levels, slopes, and possible discontinuities, not a timeless attribute. Each domain declares its expected temporal behaviour (trending, seasonally varying, shock-prone, or plausibly stable), and the definition states how the project will detect when its description of health has gone stale (see Health is not stationary).
  6. Operationalisation — for each domain, the candidate indicator families that can represent it with obtainable data — noting that data explaining BC’s health need not originate in BC (see Why BC only?).

The hard boundary: health vs. its determinants

The most common failure mode in “state of health” projects is quietly mixing health outcomes with their determinants (income, environment, service access). This project keeps them in separate layers: determinants enter the analysis as explanatory variables, never as components of health itself. The causal structures in milestone M5 depend on that distinction being drawn early.
