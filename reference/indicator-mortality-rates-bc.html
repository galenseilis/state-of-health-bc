Indicator: Mortality rates, British Columbia

Status: first published indicator (milestone M3 in progress). Regenerate with uv run sohbc build mortality_rates_bc.

This is the project’s first end-to-end indicator: it is fetched from a catalogued source, tidied by the pipeline, and published as a versioned dataset plus the views below. Each indicator page states its provenance and caveats (see the roadmap, milestone M3).

Provenance

Field Value
Domain Mortality
Definition Deaths and the mortality rate (deaths per 1,000 population), all ages, by sex (both sexes, males, females), resident in British Columbia; crude and directly age-standardised per sex (WHO World Standard Population 2000–2025) (Ahmad et al. 2001)
Source Statistics Canada, table 13-10-0710-01, Mortality rates, by age group (Statistics Canada, n.d.)
Access Statistics Canada Web Data Service (automated full-table download)
Form indicator table (published summary statistics)
Geography British Columbia, place of residence
Coverage 1991–present (annual); observation window shown on each figure
Frequency / refresh annual
Licence Statistics Canada Open Licence
Proxy note A proxy for the mortality domain, not the state of health itself (why)

The crude rate is per 1,000 residents at the observed age mix; the age-standardised rate applies the WHO World Standard Population (2000–2025) (Ahmad et al. 2001), removing the effect of BC’s ageing population. The grey band marks the COVID-19 period (2020–2024); the red dashed lines are the crude rate’s regime breaks, the blue dotted lines the age-standardised rate’s.

Trajectory and regimes

Trend summaries, recomputed on every build (data/published/mortality_rates_bc_trend.json, data/published/mortality_rates_bc_age_standardised_trend.json):

segments = trend.segments.length
  ? trend.segments
  : [{label: "whole series", start: trend.start, end: trend.end, ...trend.overall}]
Inputs.table(segments, {
  columns: ["label", "start", "end", "slope_per_year", "slope_per_decade", "fitted_start", "fitted_end", "change_within"],
  header: {label: "crude segment", start: "from", end: "to", slope_per_year: "slope/yr", slope_per_decade: "slope/decade", fitted_start: "level at start", fitted_end: "level at end", change_within: "change within"}
})
jumpRows = trend.jumps.length
  ? trend.jumps.map(j => ({year: j.year, previous_level: j.previous_level, next_level: j.next_level, jump: j.jump}))
  : [{year: "—", previous_level: "—", next_level: "—", jump: 0}]
Inputs.table(jumpRows, {
  columns: ["year", "previous_level", "next_level", "jump"],
  header: {year: "break", previous_level: "level before", next_level: "level after", jump: "level jump"}
})
dc = trend.decomposition
md`The break at **${trend.change_point}** is mostly a change of
**slope**, not of level: the total change of
${dc.observed_change.toFixed(2)} decomposes into
${dc.within_regime_change.toFixed(2)} within the regimes and
${dc.between_regime_change.toFixed(2)} in level jumps at the breaks.`
md`Over ${trend.start}–${trend.end} the crude rate changed by
**${trend.overall.slope_per_decade.toFixed(2)}** per 1,000 per decade, and the
series is **${trend.stationarity.verdict}**.
It carries a monotone trend (Kendall's tau = ${trend.trend_test.tau.toFixed(2)},
p = ${trend.trend_test.p_value.toFixed(3)}) and departure from a single straight
line (residual-permutation test, p = ${trend.break_test.p_value.toFixed(3)}).
The BIC-selected regimes break at ${trend.change_points.join(", ")}, the largest
slope change at **${trend.change_point}**${trend.change_point === 2020 ? " — the onset of the COVID-19 period" : ""}.`

The age-standardised rate tells a different story, because it is the one that abstracts from population ageing:

asrSegments = asrTrend.segments
Inputs.table(asrSegments, {
  columns: ["label", "start", "end", "slope_per_year", "slope_per_decade"],
  header: {label: "standardised segment", start: "from", end: "to", slope_per_year: "slope/yr", slope_per_decade: "slope/decade"}
})
md`The age-standardised rate is **${asrTrend.stationarity.verdict}**
(Kendall's tau = ${asrTrend.trend_test.tau.toFixed(2)},
p = ${asrTrend.trend_test.p_value.toFixed(3)}; break test
p = ${asrTrend.break_test.p_value.toFixed(3)}), with a regime break at
**${asrTrend.change_point}**: the decline reverses there — the standardised
rate fell before it and has risen since.`

Method. Regimes are dated by a piecewise-linear segmentation, solved exactly by dynamic programming, with the number of breaks chosen by BIC; whether the series departs from one straight line is tested by residual permutation (Truong et al. 2020), and the trend by Kendall’s tau. Each series is therefore labelled stationary or not over its observed window, as milestone M3 requires. The full test output, including every candidate segmentation, is in the trend JSON. The permutation test assumes exchangeable residuals — autocorrelation is not modelled — so it is a test of departure from a linear trend, not a full time-series model.

Age breakdown

The same source carries every five-year age group. Each is built into its own trajectory, so we can ask whether the decline is general or driven by particular ages (data/published/mortality_age_specific_bc_by_age_trend.json; generated by uv run sohbc build mortality_age_specific_bc).

byAge = await FileAttachment("../data/published/mortality_age_specific_bc_by_age_trend.json").json()
ageRows = rows.filter(d => d.characteristic === "Mortality rate per 1,000 population" && d.sex === "Both sexes" && d.age_group !== "Age at time of death, all ages" && d.age_group !== "Age at time of death, not stated")
ageOrder = ["under 1 year", "1 to 4 years", "5 to 9 years", "10 to 14 years", "15 to 19 years", "20 to 24 years", "25 to 29 years", "30 to 34 years", "35 to 39 years", "40 to 44 years", "45 to 49 years", "50 to 54 years", "55 to 59 years", "60 to 64 years", "65 to 69 years", "70 to 74 years", "75 to 79 years", "80 to 84 years", "85 to 89 years", "90 years and over"]
short = g => g.replace("Age at time of death, ", "")
Plot.plot({
  title: "Mortality rate by age group, both sexes (per 1,000; 5-year bands)",
  x: {label: "Year", tickFormat: "d"},
  y: {label: null, domain: ageOrder.slice().reverse()},
  color: {scheme: "YlGnBu", legend: true, label: "per 1,000"},
  marks: [
    Plot.cell(ageRows, {x: "ref_date", y: d => short(d.age_group), fill: "value", tip: true})
  ],
  width: 900
})
ageSummary = ageOrder.map(g => {
  const t = byAge["Both sexes"][`Age at time of death, ${g}`]
  return t && {
    age: g,
    slope_per_decade: t.overall.slope_per_decade,
    verdict: t.stationarity.verdict,
    change_point: t.change_point ?? "—"
  }
}).filter(Boolean)
Inputs.table(ageSummary, {
  columns: ["age", "slope_per_decade", "verdict", "change_point"],
  header: {age: "age group", slope_per_decade: "slope/decade (per 1,000)", verdict: "stationarity", change_point: "largest break"}
})
oldest = byAge["Both sexes"]["Age at time of death, 90 years and over"]
youngest = byAge["Both sexes"]["Age at time of death, 30 to 34 years"]
md`The decline is real at **every** age, but its size scales with age:
about **${oldest.overall.slope_per_decade.toFixed(1)}** per 1,000 per decade
at 90 and over, against roughly **${youngest.overall.slope_per_decade.toFixed(2)}**
at 30–34. The all-ages crude rate discussed above mixes these: an ageing
population shifts weight toward the high-rate old groups, so the all-ages
crude rate can rise even while every age-specific rate falls. This is the
same ageing signature seen in the crude-versus-standardised contrast,
decomposed by age.`

Method. Each age group is summarised by the same :func:~sohbc.indicators.trajectory routine as the headline series (piecewise-linear regimes by BIC, residual-permutation break test, Kendall-tau trend). Because there are 20 groups, the age-specific build uses 199 permutations rather than the headline 1,999; this coarsens the p-value but not the regime dating. Small counts in the youngest groups make their rates volatile, so their slopes and breaks are weakly identified.

Caveats

  • Crude vs standardised. Two series are published: the crude rate (observed age mix) and a directly age-standardised rate using the WHO World Standard Population (2000–2025) (Ahmad et al. 2001). The crude rate can rise while the standardised rate falls if the population ages — seen here, so read the two together.
  • Break detection, not explanation. The regimes are dated by a formal test (above) (Truong et al. 2020); the test says that a break beyond a single straight line exists, not what caused it, and the 2020–2024 regime rests on five annual points, so its slope is the least certain.
  • The standardised turnaround predates the pandemic. The age-standardised rate’s regime break falls at 2013, before the COVID-19 period, so the pandemic does not explain the reversal on its own; the pipeline dates the change and does not attribute it.
  • Observation window and regimes. The series spans 1991–2024 and crosses the COVID-19 period (2020–2024), marked on the figure.
  • Sex categories are the source’s. The table publishes both sexes, males, and females; the pipeline reports these categories as given and does not model sex or gender beyond them.
  • Resolution. Published at provincial resolution, with age-group rows (both sexes) and male/female breakdowns in the tidy file, and age-specific trajectories in a separate summary. Sub-provincial views are a separate indicator.
  • Revisions. Statistics Canada may revise recent-year estimates; the published files are regenerated from the source on each build.

Reproduce

uv run sohbc fetch statcan_wds
uv run sohbc build mortality_rates_bc mortality_age_specific_bc

The tidy dataset is published at data/published/mortality_rates_bc.csv (all ages and age groups, by sex), with the age-standardised series — one row per year and sex — at data/published/mortality_rates_bc_age_standardised.csv (standard population weights in data/reference/who_standard_population.csv). The headline trend tests are at data/published/mortality_rates_bc_trend.json and data/published/mortality_rates_bc_age_standardised_trend.json, and the per-sex tests at data/published/mortality_rates_bc_by_sex_trend.json.
