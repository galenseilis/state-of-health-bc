State of health in BC: a descriptive report

NoteVersion and observation window

Report version 0.4 · observation windows: mortality 1991–2024, life expectancy 1980–2024, survey indicators 2015–2024, sub-provincial mortality 2021/2023 · generated from the published datasets by quarto render. Regenerate the underlying data with uv run sohbc build before re-rendering (see Reproduce).

Numbers in this report are regenerated on every build from the files under data/published/; nothing here is typed in by hand. If a figure and this prose disagree, the figure is right.

This is an edition of the project’s “State of health in BC” descriptive report (deliverable of milestone M3 in the roadmap). It describes what the currently-ingested indicators show — not the state of health in full. The indicator set now spans mortality and survey-based morbidity, mental health, and wellbeing, and reaches below the province to the regional mortality gap; the report will keep widening, and each edition carries its observation window because the numbers are dated, not timeless (why).

Several things follow from the table, and they are the report’s core finding:

  1. Almost nothing is stationary. Nearly every series changes over its window; a single summary value would be false to almost all of them. Two — diabetes and having a regular provider — are labelled stationary over their short window, so absence of evidence is reported as such.
  2. Direction differs by measure. The crude mortality rate and life expectancy trend in opposite senses, because crude mortality is not age-adjusted while life expectancy already integrates the age pattern of death; the survey series likewise split, with wellbeing falling while self-reported poor health and mental-health conditions rise.
  3. The change is not one slope. Many series carry a regime break beyond a straight line, several dated to 2020; the breaks are the story, not noise.

Regimes are slope changes, not level shifts

A regime break can mean two different things: the series steps to a new level, or it keeps its level but changes its rate of movement. The pipeline now separates the two, and for almost every indicator it is the second.

decomp = [
  {series: "Mortality rate (crude)", d: mort},
  {series: "Life expectancy at birth", d: life},
  {series: "Life satisfaction", d: tLifeSat},
  {series: "Sense of belonging", d: tBelong},
  {series: "Anxiety disorder", d: tAnx},
  {series: "Mood disorder", d: tMood},
  {series: "Perceived mental health, fair or poor", d: tMentH},
  {series: "Perceived health, fair or poor", d: tPhyH},
  {series: "Daily smoker", d: tSmoke},
  {series: "High blood pressure", d: tBp},
  {series: "Diabetes", d: tDiab},
  {series: "Has a regular provider", d: tProv}
].map(({series, d}) => ({
  series,
  observed: d.decomposition.observed_change,
  within: d.decomposition.within_regime_change,
  between: d.decomposition.between_regime_change,
  share_between: d.decomposition.observed_change !== 0
    ? Math.abs(d.decomposition.between_regime_change) / (Math.abs(d.decomposition.within_regime_change) + Math.abs(d.decomposition.between_regime_change))
    : 0,
  jumps: d.jumps.map(j => `${j.year}: ${j.jump.toFixed(2)}`).join("; ") || "—"
}))
Inputs.table(decomp, {
  columns: ["series", "observed", "within", "between", "jumps"],
  header: {series: "series", observed: "total change", within: "within-regime", between: "between-regime (jumps)", jumps: "jumps (year: level shift)"},
  width: {jumps: 200}
})
biggest = decomp.filter(d => d.jumps !== "—").sort((a, b) => Math.abs(b.between) - Math.abs(a.between))
top = biggest[0]
md`The "between-regime" column is nearly zero for all but one series. The
largest level shift anywhere is life expectancy at 2015
(**${life.jumps[0].jump.toFixed(2)}** years), and even there the bulk of the
change is within-regime (${life.decomposition.within_regime_change.toFixed(2)}
of ${life.decomposition.observed_change.toFixed(2)}). For the survey series
the jumps are fractions of a point. So when this report says a series'
"rules change", it means the **slope** changes: the direction and pace of
movement turn, but the series does not lurch discontinuously to a new
level. That is the more common — and more policy-relevant — kind of
nonstationarity.`

Mortality: an ageing population masks a turning point

rows = FileAttachment("../data/published/mortality_rates_bc.csv").csv({typed: true})
rates = rows.filter(d => d.characteristic === "Mortality rate per 1,000 population" && d.age_group === "Age at time of death, all ages" && d.sex === "Both sexes")
asr = FileAttachment("../data/published/mortality_rates_bc_age_standardised.csv").csv({typed: true}).filter(d => d.sex === "Both sexes")
series = rates.map(d => ({year: d.ref_date, rate: d.value, series: "Crude"}))
  .concat(asr.map(d => ({year: d.ref_date, rate: d.value, series: "Age-standardised"})))
Plot.plot({
  title: "Mortality rate, British Columbia (per 1,000 population)",
  x: {label: "Year", tickFormat: "d"},
  y: {grid: true, label: "Deaths per 1,000 population"},
  color: {legend: true},
  marks: [
    Plot.rectX([{x1: 2020, x2: 2024}], {x1: "x1", x2: "x2", fill: "grey", fillOpacity: 0.12}),
    Plot.ruleX(mort.change_points, {stroke: "red", strokeDasharray: "4,4"}),
    Plot.ruleX(mortAsr.change_points, {stroke: "steelblue", strokeDasharray: "2,3"}),
    Plot.lineY(series, {x: "year", y: "rate", stroke: "series", tip: true}),
    Plot.dot(series, {x: "year", y: "rate", stroke: "series", tip: true})
  ]
})
md`Over ${mort.start}–${mort.end} the **crude** rate rose by
**${mort.overall.slope_per_decade.toFixed(2)}** per 1,000 per decade, while the
**age-standardised** rate, which removes the effect of population ageing,
changed by **${mortAsr.overall.slope_per_decade.toFixed(2)}** — i.e. it *fell*
on net. The crude rise is largely the ageing of the population, not a
worsening of age-specific risk. The age-standardised series is the one that
abstracts from that: it declined from 1991 and its regime turned at
**${mortAsr.change_point}**, after which the decline flattened and
reversed. The pink band marks the COVID-19 period (2020–2024).`

The gap between the two lines is the signature of demographic ageing, and it is exactly why the project reports both and treats the standardised series as the one about risk rather than the one about the age structure. The age breakdown makes the mechanism explicit: the decline is real at every age, but it scales with age (about 9.7 per 1,000 per decade at 90 and over against 0.00 at 30–34), so a rising all-ages crude rate coexists with falling rates at every age. The full breakdown is on the mortality indicator page.

md`Life expectancy at birth (both sexes) rose by
**${life.overall.slope_per_decade.toFixed(2)}** years per decade on average
across ${life.start}–${life.end}, but as two regimes: gains of about
**${life.segments[0].slope_per_decade.toFixed(2)}** years per decade to
**${life.change_point}**, then a flat-to-declining
**${life.segments[1].slope_per_decade.toFixed(2)}** years per decade after.
The post-2015 stall spans the COVID-19 period, which is visible as the
2020–2022 dip, but the regime turn **precedes** the pandemic — the pipeline
dates the change and does not attribute it.`

This is the project’s nonstationarity claim in one series: the same indicator, the same source, but a different rule before and after 2015. The male and female series turn differently (below), so even this agreement about whether there was a break comes with disagreement about when.

Beyond mortality: wellbeing down, distress up

The survey family widens the report past death. Over 2015–2024, the wellbeing series fall and the self-reported distress series rise, and several breaks land on 2020.

Inequalities across regions

The sub-provincial view is now published for mortality. Using Statistics Canada table 13-10-0931-01 (three-year averages, 2021/2023), the age-standardised premature-mortality rate varies widely across BC’s health service delivery areas.

prem = disp.dispersion["Premature mortality"]["Both sexes"]
avoid = disp.dispersion["Potentially avoidable mortality"]["Both sexes"]
regions = prem.regions.map(r => ({region: r.region.replace(" Health Service Delivery Area", ""), value: r.value}))
Plot.plot({
  title: `Age-standardised premature mortality by health service delivery area, ${prem.ref_date} (per 100,000)`,
  marginLeft: 170,
  x: {grid: true, label: "Premature deaths per 100,000"},
  y: {label: null, domain: regions.map(d => d.region)},
  marks: [
    Plot.barX(regions, {y: "region", x: "value", fill: "value", tip: true}),
    Plot.ruleX([prem.mean], {stroke: "black", strokeDasharray: "4,4"})
  ],
  color: {scheme: "Reds", legend: false}
})
md`Across **${prem.n_regions}** health service delivery areas, premature
mortality ranges from **${prem.lowest.value.toFixed(1)}** in
${prem.lowest.region.replace(" Health Service Delivery Area", "")} to
**${prem.highest.value.toFixed(1)}** in
${prem.highest.region.replace(" Health Service Delivery Area", "")} — a
**${prem.ratio.toFixed(1)}×** gap, against a provincial average of about
${prem.mean.toFixed(0)}. Potentially avoidable mortality shows the same
pattern (${avoid.ratio.toFixed(1)}×). The dashed line is the mean across
areas. The northern and interior service delivery areas are consistently
highest, the Vancouver-metro areas lowest.`

This is a genuine inequality signal, and it is exactly what a provincial-average report hides. Two limits apply. First, the source is three-year averages and covers only two overlapping periods, so it supports this cross-sectional snapshot but not a regional trajectory — the trend machinery is not run on it. Second, the rate confidence intervals overlap between neighbouring regions, so the ordering at the middle of the distribution is not sharp; the roughly 3× gap between the extremes is. No causal claim is made here: the regional pattern co-moves with age structure, remoteness, and service access, which are M4/M5 questions.

Reading this report

  • Dates are the point. Every number above belongs to its window; reproducing the pipeline on refreshed data may change the verdicts.
  • Proxies, several domains. Mortality and life expectancy are mortality-domain proxies; the survey family adds self-reported morbidity, mental health, wellbeing, and access to care. None is the state of health itself, and a self-report is not a diagnosis.
  • Break detection is not explanation. The dated regimes come from a formal test of departure from a straight line (Truong et al. 2020); the test says where the rule changes, not why. Attribution is M4/M5 work.
  • Aggregates hide sub-series. A both-sexes series is not a summary of the male and female trajectories; read them separately.
  • Survey versus administrative. The mortality series are administrative (complete counts), the survey series are samples: the latter carry sampling error, suppression, and a mid-series collection change, so a survey step may be measurement, not change.
